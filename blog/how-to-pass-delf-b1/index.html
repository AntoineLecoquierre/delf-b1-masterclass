DELF B1 Guide

How to Pass the DELF B1: the complete guide

Antoine L. Native French Teacher, Paris 9 min read

The DELF B1 is very passable — if you understand how it is built. Most people who fail don't fail because their French is weak; they fail because no one showed them what the exam actually rewards. This guide walks you through the format, the scoring, and exactly how to prepare each of the four sections.

First, understand what the DELF B1 really is

The DELF (Diplôme d'Études en Langue Française) is the official French-language diploma issued by France Éducation international for the French Ministry of Education. The B1 level corresponds to an independent user on the CEFR scale: you can handle everyday situations, express an opinion, and cope with most things that come up while travelling or living in a French-speaking country.

Two facts change how you should prepare:

  • The diploma is valid for life — you pass once and it never expires.
  • The exam is identical in structure everywhere in the world. Once you know the format, there are no surprises on exam day.

The format and scoring, in one table

There are four sections. Each is marked out of 25, for a total of 100. This is the single most important thing to memorise:

The rule that trips people up: you pass at 50/100 overall, but you also need a minimum of 5/25 in every single section. Score 4/25 on speaking and you fail — even with 70/100 total. This is why you can never ignore a skill you dislike.

Section 1 — Listening

You hear three recordings, from short announcements to a longer interview or report. Each recording is usually played twice, with time to read the questions first.

  • Read the questions before the audio starts. They tell you what to listen for — a number, a reason, an opinion — so you're hunting for specific information, not translating every word.
  • Use the first listen for the gist, the second for the details. Don't panic if you miss something the first time; that's what the second play is for.
  • Train with numbers, dates and times. Prices, phone numbers and opening hours are classic questions, and they're easy points once your ear is used to them.

Section 2 — Reading

You read two documents — often a practical text (an email, a notice, a website) and a longer article — and answer comprehension questions, including true/false with justification.

  • Skim first, then scan. Read the whole text quickly for the general meaning, then go back and hunt for the exact detail each question asks about.
  • For true/false, always justify with a quotation. The exam usually asks you to copy the sentence that proves your answer — no quote, no marks, even if the box is ticked correctly.
  • Watch for paraphrase. The right answer rarely uses the same words as the text; it restates them. Learning synonyms is half the battle.

Section 3 — Writing

You write one essay of 160–180 words giving your opinion on a familiar topic (the environment, technology, life in your country, and so on). More than half the marks here are for content and coherence, not grammar — which is great news.

Use a simple four-paragraph structure that always works:

  1. Introduction: rephrase the question and state your position.
  2. Argument 1 + a concrete example.
  3. Argument 2 + a concrete example (ideally a nuance or the other side).
  4. Conclusion: restate your opinion — don't add new ideas.
Fast wins: pack your essay with linking words (tout d'abord, par exemple, cependant, pour conclure). A simple essay that is on-topic, the right length and well-organised outscores a "clever" one that rambles. And count your words — under 160 is penalised.

Section 4 — Speaking

The oral has three parts, run back-to-back with an examiner:

  • Guided interview — a short conversation about you. Prepare a 40-second self-presentation that naturally uses the past, present and future.
  • Interaction / role-play — you solve a situation with the examiner (booking, negotiating, convincing). Drill the phrases for asking, refusing politely and proposing.
  • Expressing a point of view — you draw a short document, get 10 minutes to prepare, then talk for a few minutes and answer questions.
  • Keep talking and self-correct out loud. Fluency counts more than perfection; silence costs you more than a small mistake you fix yourself.
  • Record yourself. Hearing your own answers is the single fastest way to improve — and it removes exam-day nerves.

A realistic study plan

You don't need months. With an A2–B1 level, 4 to 8 weeks at around 30–45 minutes a day is enough if you train all four skills and, crucially, practise with real exam-style papers. A sensible rhythm:

  • Weeks 1–2: master the format and drill listening + reading with timed papers.
  • Weeks 3–4: writing — learn the structure, write two full essays, correct them against a model.
  • Weeks 5–6: speaking — rehearse all three parts aloud and record them.
  • Final week: a full timed mock exam, then target only your weakest section.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Ignoring your weakest skill. The 5/25 rule means one neglected section can sink everything.
  • Leaving blanks. There's no negative marking — always attempt every question and never hand in an empty essay box.
  • Studying French in general instead of the exam. The DELF rewards specific techniques; practising the exam format is what moves your score.
  • Never doing a timed mock. Time pressure is part of the test. Simulate it at least once before the real thing.

Exam-day checklist

  • Read every instruction twice and manage your time per section.
  • Attempt every question — no blanks.
  • In speaking, keep talking and correct yourself out loud.
  • Breathe. If you've trained all four skills, you're ready.

Frequently asked questions

What score do you need to pass the DELF B1?

50 out of 100 overall — with a minimum of 5/25 in each of the four sections. A score below 5 in any one section is an automatic fail, even if your total is above 50.

How long does it take to prepare?

With an A2–B1 level, most learners need 4 to 8 weeks of focused practice (around 30–45 minutes a day), training all four skills with real exam-style papers.

Is the DELF B1 valid forever?

Yes — the DELF is an official diploma from France Éducation international and is valid for life. It never expires.
