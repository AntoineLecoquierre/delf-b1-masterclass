DELF B1 Listening

DELF B1 Listening: tips & strategy to score higher

Antoine L. Native French Teacher, Paris 7 min read

Listening feels like the section you can't control — the audio plays, and either you catch it or you don't. In reality it's one of the most trainable parts of the DELF B1, because the questions are predictable and you almost always hear each recording twice. Here's the strategy that turns panic into points.

How the listening test works

You hear three recordings, from a short announcement or message to a longer interview or report. The whole section lasts about 25 minutes and is marked out of 25. Most recordings are played twice, and you get time to read the questions before each one starts.

Read the questions before the audio

This is the single biggest lever. Before a word is spoken, the questions tell you what to listen for — a price, a reason, a time, an opinion. So you're not translating everything; you're hunting for specific information. Underline the key word in each question (quand ? combien ? pourquoi ?) so your ear knows its target.

The two-listen strategy

  • First listen — the gist. Understand the situation: who is speaking, where, about what. Don't panic if you miss a detail; that's what the second play is for. Tick the answers you're sure of.
  • Second listen — the details. Now confirm the exact answers and fill the gaps you left. Focus your attention on the questions you couldn't answer the first time.
Never leave a blank. There's no negative marking. If you're unsure after the second listen, make your best guess — a plausible answer can still be right.

Train your ear for numbers & details

Prices, phone numbers, dates, times and quantities are classic questions — and easy points once your ear is used to them. French numbers trip up learners (soixante-quinze, quatre-vingt-dix), so drill them until they're instant.

  • Practise writing numbers, times and dates from audio every day.
  • Learn the "signpost" words that flag the answer: à partir de, jusqu'à, environ, sauf, à cause de, grâce à.
  • Watch for paraphrase: the answer rarely uses the exact word from the audio — it restates it. Synonyms are half the game.

Common traps to avoid

  • The first number you hear isn't always the answer. Speakers often correct themselves (« à 8h… non, finalement à 9h »). Wait for the confirmed detail.
  • Negations flip meaning. A tiny ne… pas / plus / jamais changes everything — listen to the whole sentence.
  • Don't over-write. While you're busy writing a long note, you miss the next sentence. Jot keywords, not sentences.

How to practise between now and exam day

Little and often beats cramming. Listen to short, natural French every day — learner podcasts, the news in slow French, short videos — and actively note the numbers and key facts. Then, at least once a week, sit a real exam-style recording with the printed questions under timed conditions, and correct yourself with the answer key so you learn from every trap.

Frequently asked questions

How many times do you hear each recording?

Most are played twice, with time to read the questions first. Use the first listen for the gist, the second for the details.

How long is the listening section?

About 25 minutes across three recordings, marked out of 25.

How can I improve my listening quickly?

Listen to short, natural French daily and note numbers and dates, then practise with real exam-style recordings and their questions.
